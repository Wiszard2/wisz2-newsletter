GTM 101 for LP Solution Providers: Selling Into Risk-Averse Buyers

The LP buying committee isn't shrinking and it isn't getting less cautious. Here's what the 2026 buyer-behavior data actually says about that committee — and the GTM motion that wins it anyway.

Published Tuesday, October 20, 2026 · 6–8 min read
TLDR
  • The buying committee isn't 2 people anymore — it's closer to 20. A typical B2B tech purchase now involves 10–13 internal stakeholders plus up to 9 external participants, and 70% of that buying journey happens before a seller is ever contacted.
  • 94% of buying groups rank their vendor shortlist before contacting sales — and the #1-ranked vendor from that shortlist wins the deal roughly 80% of the time. Most GTM motions are decided before the first call, not during it.
  • Risk aversion isn't paranoia, it's informed. Retail data breaches have nearly doubled and third-party vendor incidents now drive a growing share of losses — every new LP vendor a buyer evaluates is also a new attack surface they have to underwrite.
  • 86% of B2B purchases stall somewhere in the process, and 81% of buyers end up dissatisfied with the vendor they picked anyway — which means the solution providers who fix friction, not just features, are the ones actually closing.

The committee got bigger. The budget got slower to move. Nobody told sales.

A lot of GTM playbooks in this category still assume a 2018 buyer: a champion, maybe a budget-holder, a handful of emails, a demo, a close. That buyer doesn't exist anymore, in LP or anywhere else in B2B. A typical enterprise software purchase now involves 10 to 13 internal stakeholders plus up to 9 external participants — IT, finance, operations, legal, end users, each scoring the deal against a different rubric — and AI-related purchases can pull in 20 or more. By the time a solution provider's rep gets a meeting, roughly 70% of that buying journey has already happened without them.

That's not a sales-process failure. It's the buyer doing exactly what Forrester and Corporate Visions' research says buyers are increasingly forced to do: 79% of B2B purchases now require CFO approval, 87% of tech buyers have restricted their process to mission-critical purchases only, and 92% of buyers start evaluation with at least one vendor already in mind — most of them having already built a day-one shortlist of 3 to 4 vendors they'll seriously consider. The committee isn't being slow for its own sake. It's protecting itself from a bad decision it will own for years.

D&D Daily's own cybersecurity coverage this year is a useful reminder of exactly what that caution is protecting against: retail data breaches are described as nearly doubling, and Willis's cyber-insurance loss data shows third-party vendor incidents now account for a growing share of total losses. Every new LP vendor a retailer brings in isn't just a line item — it's a new vendor relationship their security and legal teams have to underwrite. A solution provider who shows up only with a product demo, and no answer for "how do you handle our data, and who else has trusted you with theirs," is asking a risk-averse buyer to absorb risk they were never going to absorb casually.

Four numbers every GTM leader selling into this buyer should have memorized:

  • 10–13internal stakeholders in a typical B2B tech purchase, plus up to 9 external participants — 20+ for AI-related purchases.6sense, 2025 B2B Buyer Experience Report
  • 70%of the buying journey happens before a seller is ever contacted.6sense, 2025
  • 94%of buying groups rank their vendor shortlist before contacting sales — the #1-ranked vendor wins the deal about 80% of the time.Corporate Visions, citing 6Sense, 2025
  • 86%of B2B purchases stall somewhere in the process, and 81% of buyers are dissatisfied with the vendor they end up choosing anyway.Forrester, 2024, via Corporate Visions

Read together, these numbers say something sharper than "buyers are cautious." They say most of the deal is won or lost before a rep ever opens a discovery call — in the content, references, and peer signal the buying committee finds on its own — and that even the deals that do close often leave the buyer unsatisfied, which is its own kind of risk for whoever's trying to land an expansion or a renewal later.

The reframe: the friction is the product, not an obstacle to it

Emblaze's research puts a number on a problem every LP solution-provider rep has felt but rarely measured: sellers and buyers show an average 54.5% misalignment on what the buyer's actual core problem even is. Buyers change their own problem statement an average of 3.2 times during a complex purchase, as more of the committee weighs in. The sellers who build in a problem-statement checkpoint — revisiting and reconfirming it every time a new stakeholder joins — see win rates improve by 38%. That's not a soft-skills tip. It's the GTM equivalent of exception-based reporting: stop assuming the first signal you got is still the right one, and build a checkpoint that catches it when it drifts.

Friction compounds the same way. SBI's research found high-friction buying environments cut the odds of a purchase happening at all by 43%, and 71% of buyers describe their experience with supplier reps as outright frustrating. Gartner's 2025 data backs that up from the buyer's side: 61% of B2B buyers now prefer a rep-free buying experience where possible, and 73% actively avoid vendors that send irrelevant outreach. None of that means solution providers should sell less actively — it means the selling has to show up as removing friction for the committee, not adding to it. A forwardable one-page ROI summary a champion can hand to their CFO without editing it first does more for a deal than another check-in call. A reference call set up before it's asked for does more than a glossy case study PDF.

The through-line across every issue of this newsletter has been the same: the winners aren't the ones with the loudest signal, they're the ones who build the system that filters to the few signals that actually matter and act on them fast. That's true of exception-based reporting catching the handful of transactions worth investigating out of millions. It's equally true of a GTM motion that filters a 20-person buying committee down to the 3 or 4 stakeholders actually driving the decision, and gives each of them exactly the proof they need to move it forward.

If you're building or leading a GTM motion for an LP solution provider

Three shifts separate the teams that are winning this buyer from the ones still running a 2018 playbook against a 2026 committee. First, multi-thread every open deal — not as a hedge against a champion leaving, but because the committee's 10-plus stakeholders are each going to independently veto a deal that doesn't address their specific concern, whether or not your champion ever surfaces it. Second, arm the champion, don't just sell to them: a one-pager with real ROI math, a peer reference, and a plain-language answer to the security/vendor-risk question does more to move a stalled deal than another feature walkthrough. Third, build the problem-statement checkpoint into your own sales process, not just your messaging — revisit it explicitly every time a new stakeholder enters the deal, because the version of the problem your champion described in week one is rarely the version the CFO is going to fund in week twelve.

The move this week

Three things worth auditing in your current pipeline, not your next one:

  • Map the actual buying committee on every deal past discovery — not just the champion. If you can't name the finance, security, and end-user stakeholders by name, you don't have the deal mapped, you have a contact.
  • Build one forwardable, CFO-ready one-pager per deal type — ROI math, a peer reference, and a plain answer to the vendor-risk question — and hand it to the champion before they have to ask for it.
  • Add a problem-statement checkpoint at every stage gate. If the buyer's stated problem hasn't been reconfirmed since a new stakeholder joined, you're selling against a stale brief.
