The RFP Season Playbook: What Retail LP Buyers Actually Score On

Most RFPs get scored before a single demo happens, on a rubric the vendor never sees. Here's what the data actually says retail LP buyers weight — and the shortlist mistakes that cost solution providers the deal.

Published Tuesday, November 10, 2026 · 5–7 min read
TLDR
  • Retail security budgets keep climbing into 2027 — 57% of large retailers say theft-prevention budgets are up over the last two years, and 64% call theft a "crisis point." That's more RFPs hitting more inboxes this quarter, not fewer.
  • 94% of B2B buying groups rank their vendor shortlist before they ever talk to a seller — and the #1-ranked vendor wins roughly 80% of the time. By the time the RFP document lands in your inbox, most of the scoring logic is already set.
  • Price is rarely the top-weighted line. Procurement's own best-practice guidance says capabilities and experience are usually weighted heaviest — and in retail LP specifically, implementation ownership and in-band references score higher than feature checklists.
  • The providers who win RFP season aren't the ones with the longest feature list. They're the ones who show up with a measurable outcome story, references in the same vertical and store-count band, and a straight answer on false positives — before anyone asks.

It's RFP season, and most solution providers are about to lose on criteria they never saw

November and December are when next year's retail tech budget stops being a slide in a deck and starts being a line in a contract. The $113B 2026 U.S. retail tech number isn't an abstraction right now — it's the number finance is allocating against real RFPs, this month, for work that starts in Q1.

And the pressure behind those RFPs isn't easing. A 2024 Avery Dennison-commissioned survey of 300 senior retail leaders found 64% describe theft as having hit a "crisis point," and 57% say their theft-prevention budgets are up over the prior two years — with 33% still saying the resources aren't enough. That's an industry that keeps writing bigger checks for loss prevention technology, not smaller ones. More budget pressure plus more urgency equals more RFPs — which is exactly why it's worth knowing how they actually get scored before yours lands in one.

Here's the part most solution providers miss: by the time a formal RFP goes out, the buying committee has usually already done most of the scoring in their heads. Corporate Visions, citing 6Sense's 2025 B2B Buyer Experience research, found that 94% of buying groups rank their vendor shortlist before contacting a single seller — and the vendor ranked #1 going in wins the deal roughly 80% of the time. The RFP isn't the contest. It's the confirmation of a contest that mostly already happened.

None of this is a universal, independently audited scoring formula for every retail LP RFP — nobody's published one. But three separate sources, from three different angles, point the same direction: the buyers scoring these deals care more about proof and experience than about who has the lowest line-item price or the longest feature list.

The reframe: the RFP is scored on proof, not features

Read enough retail LP RFP templates and a pattern shows up in what buyers actually ask for, beyond the standard feature matrix: how will success be measured — shrink basis points, recovery value, incident closure time, false-alarm rate, not just "does it detect theft"; who owns hardware installation, data integration, and investigator training during rollout; what references exist in your exact vertical and store-count band, not just "retail" broadly; and whether professional services, monitoring, and hardware maintenance are quoted separately or buried inside a bundled number. The same template flags the red flags buyers are trained to watch for: no references in your store-count band, a generic shrink dashboard with no POS correlation, an inability to state your own false-positive rate, and case-management tools with no mobile capture for store teams.

That matches the general procurement guidance almost exactly: publish your scoring weights before responses come in, score individually before the group discusses, and don't let the highest raw score be the automatic winner — budget and switching costs still move the decision at the end. A solution provider walking into that process with a features-first pitch is answering a question nobody on the buying committee is actually asking anymore.

If you're a solution provider walking into RFP season

Three shifts worth making before your next response goes out, not after it's scored:

  • Lead with your outcome-measurement methodology, not your price. If you can't state your own false-positive rate and how you've measured shrink-bps improvement in past deployments, that's the gap buyers are screening for first.
  • Bring references in the exact vertical and store-count band you're bidding into. A reference customer outside that band reads as a gap, not a credential, in the scoring data above.
  • Separate software, hardware, and professional-services costs in writing before anyone asks. Bundled or hidden hardware/tag costs are the single most-cited commercial red flag in retail LP RFP scoring — and the easiest one to avoid.

The move this week

Whichever side of the table you're sitting on this quarter:

  • Solution providers: pull your last three lost RFPs and check whether the real loss reason was a feature gap or a missing reference/implementation proof point. It's usually the second one.
  • Write down your own answer to "how will success be measured" in writing, before a buyer ever asks — then make sure your next three proposals actually lead with it.
  • LP buyers: before the RFP goes out, get a vendor-neutral read on your current system first. If the gap is usage, not capability, the RFP you're about to write might be solving the wrong problem.
