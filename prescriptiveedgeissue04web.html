The Data Foundation Every LP Program Needs Before It Buys More Cameras

Most retailers don't have a camera problem. They have a footage problem — and AI is what turns thousands of hours of video into the handful of incidents that actually matter.

Published Tuesday, September 29, 2026 · 5–7 min read
TLDR
  • A single 2026 benchmark study analyzed 1.6 million monitoring events across more than 18,000 U.S. retail locations. No LP team is reviewing that volume of footage by hand — and no LP team needs to.
  • 95% of alarm events verified against video turn out to be false alarms (Interface Systems, 2026). That's not a camera problem. It's a signal problem — exactly what AI-driven video analysis is built to fix.
  • 67% of retailers already run AI-based video analytics to flag suspicious behavior. Adoption isn't the same as a working data foundation — the AI only earns its keep once it's correlated against POS data.
  • Well-implemented AI exception detection is cutting shrink 20–30% where retailers have gotten that foundation right — proof the value was never in the camera. It's in what the AI does with what the camera sees.

The alert flood nobody talks about

A 2026 benchmark study analyzed 1.6 million monitoring events across more than 18,000 U.S. retail locations and 51 brands. No retailer reading this has a team that reviews footage at that scale by hand — and the honest answer is that most of what a camera captures was never worth a human's attention in the first place.

Here's the number that proves it: once alarm events actually get verified against video, 95% turn out to be false alarms. That's not a failure of the cameras. It's what happens when a system generates alerts without a way to tell which ones matter. More cameras just means more of the same noise, faster.

The retailers pulling ahead aren't the ones buying more lenses. They're the ones who've built a system that watches every feed continuously, cross-references it against what's actually happening at the register, and only escalates the handful of incidents a human needs to see.

Put those together and the pitch writes itself: the volume proves cameras alone create noise, not signal. The shrink number proves the fix is real, once the data foundation underneath the cameras is actually right.

The reframe: it was never about more cameras

Every camera RFP gets pitched the same way: more coverage, higher resolution, more angles. None of that answers the question that actually matters, which is what happens to the footage after it's captured. A camera that records everything and flags nothing is a storage bill, not a loss-prevention program.

AI's real value isn't detection — it's analysis and alerting done correctly. It's the layer that watches every feed continuously, joins what the camera sees with what the POS system logs, and turns thousands of hours of raw video into a short, trustworthy list of incidents worth a human's time. That correlation layer is where the margin recovery actually lives, not in the camera count.

If you're a retail technology company reading this

Retail buyers are starting to ask a different question than they did even a year ago. It's no longer "how many cameras can you support" — it's "how do you turn footage into an alert I can actually trust." Vendors who can show a real false-positive rate and a working video-to-data correlation architecture are winning deals that vendors with only a detection claim are losing. If your sales conversation still leads with camera specs, that's worth revisiting before your next RFP cycle.

The move this week

Three things worth doing before your next AI or vendor conversation, not after:

  • Ask your video or AI vendor for their real false-positive rate — not just their detection claim. If they can't give you a number, that's the answer.
  • Check whether your video system already talks to your POS or transaction data. If it doesn't, that gap — not camera count — is the actual bottleneck.
  • Pick one store to pilot a correlated video-and-data alert workflow before your next camera RFP locks in more of the same.
